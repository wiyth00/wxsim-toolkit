.SHORT TERM...
(Today through Sunday)
Issued at 815 AM EDT Sat Apr 17 2027

It is rare for this office to describe a severe weather day in north and central Georgia as a dryline event, but that is the most accurate description of what we expect this afternoon. The thermodynamic profile over the Piedmont this morning has far more in common with a spring day in West Texas or western Oklahoma than with a typical Gulf-fed Southeast setup, and forecast impacts, storm structure, and messaging all need to reflect that.

The 12Z FFC sounding tells most of the story. A shallow moist layer only about 70 mb deep (sfc temp/dewpoint 62/58F) sits beneath a sharp elevated mixed layer (EML) based near 830 mb, where the temperature jumps to +15C. Above that, the 700-500 mb lapse rate is 8.4 C/km. That value sits near the top of the FFC sounding climatology for mid-April. Lapse rates like this come from EML plumes off the Mexican Plateau, which normally stay over the southern Plains. This plume was advected east across the northern Gulf over the past 36 hours and now sits directly over the region. The cap is stout this morning, with SBCIN near -620 J/kg, but the convective temperature is only about 85F. PW is 0.69 inch, below normal for the date, which highlights how little Gulf moisture has returned since the cold front earlier this week. The wet-bulb zero height is near 8,100 ft AGL, close to ideal for large hail reaching the ground with limited melting.

Aloft, a potent shortwave trough is taking on a negative tilt over the Ozarks and Mid-South this morning. A 75-85 kt 500 mb speed max will nose into Alabama and Georgia by early afternoon, and a lead impulse at the base of the trough crosses north Georgia between roughly 3 PM and 5 PM. Upper-level divergence is enhanced by a coupled jet structure. North Georgia sits in the right-entrance region of a 130 kt polar jet streak over the Ohio Valley and the left-exit region of a subtropical jet streak along the Gulf Coast. At the surface, a 991 mb low over western Kentucky deepens toward southern Indiana through this evening. The true cold front lags well back in Mississippi and western Alabama.

The most important surface feature today is the dryline. It is a hybrid boundary driven by three processes working together:

  1) Dry continental air in the dry slot wrapping around the cyclone and surging east along a Pacific front.

  2) Deep afternoon mixing into the EML, which entrains the shallow moist layer from above and causes the boundary to jump east faster than advection alone would move it.

  3) Downslope flow off the Cumberland Plateau and southern Appalachians, adding compressional warming and drying across northwest Georgia. This supports a lee trough and a weak mesolow along the boundary.

The dryline was near I-65 in central Alabama at 8 AM. It should reach the Alabama/Georgia line from Columbus to Rome by around 2 PM, with a bulge developing near Carrollton and LaGrange beneath the nose of the mid-level speed max. By 5 PM, we expect it along a line from Americus to Griffin to the western Atlanta suburbs to Calhoun, with the bulge pushing into Fayette and Clayton counties. Behind it, temperatures climb to 88-90F with dewpoints in the upper 30s to low 40s and southwest gusts of 35-45 mph. That is closer to the Permian Basin than to the Chattahoochee Valley.

The second key boundary is the remnant cold air damming wedge from Friday. Stratus and temperatures in the low 60s persist across northeast Georgia this morning. The wedge will erode slowly from the southwest through the day. By early afternoon, its leading edge should run roughly from Gainesville to Athens to Washington, then lift slowly northeast through the evening. North and east of the boundary, the air remains cool and stable. Just south of it, moisture pools (dewpoints 62-65F) and surface winds back to the east-southeast beneath 40-45 kt of south-southwest flow at 850 mb. This creates a narrow corridor, perhaps 30 to 40 miles wide, where low-level shear and moisture are both maximized.

Convective evolution: Diurnal heating to the convective temperature, combined with cooling of the 850-700 mb layer as the lead impulse arrives, should erode the cap from the west by early afternoon. Expect agitated cumulus along the dryline bulge and near the northwest Georgia mesolow between 1 PM and 3 PM. Discrete supercells are then expected to form from roughly Carrollton to the south and west Atlanta metro to Cartersville between 3 PM and 5 PM. The FFC sounding modified for 86/58F yields MLCAPE near 2500 J/kg with mixed-layer LCLs near 1900 m. Effective bulk shear is 60-65 kt and the hodograph is long and nearly straight above 2-3 km, which favors splitting storms. The left-moving splits will be dangerous hail producers in their own right and will move quickly northeast at 50-60 mph. Right-movers will track east-northeast at around 40-45 mph.

With 50-70 kt anvil-level storm-relative flow, low PW, and a very dry subcloud layer, many of these storms are expected to be low-precipitation (LP) supercells. That means visible, rain-free bases, striated structures, and hail falling on the edges of the storm or ahead of it. Radar reflectivity may understate how dangerous some of these storms are. We will be leaning on dual-pol hail signatures, MESH, and spotter reports rather than raw reflectivity.

Hazards by type:

Hail: This is the headline threat. Deep CAPE through the -10 to -30C hail growth zone, broad and intense updrafts, and strong deep-layer shear favor long hailstone residence times. SHIP values near 3 are forecast across the metro and points south and east. Hail of 3 to 4 inches in diameter is realistic with the strongest storms, and an isolated stone larger than 4 inches is possible near the dryline bulge.

Tornadoes: Near the dryline, high LCLs and a mixed-out low-level jet will limit the tornado threat through early evening, though it is not zero. The main tornado window runs from roughly 6 PM to 11 PM as storms that formed near the metro reach the wedge-boundary corridor. There, LCLs fall to 800-900 m and 0-1 km SRH increases to 250-300 m2/s2. Both the RRFS and the REFS mean show effective-layer STP of 4-6 along this corridor. The low-level jet strengthens to 50-55 kt after 8 PM, which lengthens low-level hodographs just as storms ingest backed, moist inflow. Storms that cross well north of the boundary will become elevated, producing hail but likely not tornadoes.

Dry microbursts: West of the dryline, forecast soundings show a deep inverted-V profile to near 650 mb with DCAPE of 1500-1700 J/kg and temperature/dewpoint spreads of 40-50F. High-based storms along the dryline mixing zone and the trailing Pacific front may produce evaporatively driven downbursts of 60-80 mph between 3 PM and 8 PM, often with nothing more than virga reaching the ground. These gusts will also fan any fires that start.

Uncertainties: The biggest forecast question is how far east the dryline mixes. The RRFS is about 20-30 miles farther east than the REFS mean and the ECMWF, and the RRFS has tended to overmix in these setups. If it is correct, initiation shifts to the east metro, dewpoints mix down, and the tornado corridor narrows or is lost. Overnight convection over the northern Gulf is weakening on GOES imagery, so a cutoff of the return flow looks less likely than it did yesterday. Wedge erosion timing matters too. A faster retreat weakens the backed low-level flow, while a slower one sends storms into stable air sooner. Finally, the cap may limit storm coverage to only a few storms. That does not lower the ceiling. The storms that do form will have little competition, and any single one of them could produce the most significant hail or tornado of the day.

We will launch a special 18Z sounding. Birmingham and Greenville-Spartanburg are expected to do the same.

Storms should exit northeast Georgia by 1 to 2 AM. The cold front clears the area overnight with lows in the upper 40s to mid 50s. Sunday will be sunny, breezy, and much cooler, with highs in the upper 60s to mid 70s. Afternoon humidity drops to 20-25 percent again, so fire weather concerns continue into Sunday.

.LONG TERM...
(Sunday night through next Friday)
Issued at 402 AM EDT Sat Apr 17 2027

Quiet and dry conditions return Sunday night through at least midweek as surface high pressure builds in behind the departing system. A cool start Monday morning, with lows in the low to mid 40s and upper 30s in the mountains. A gradual warming trend follows, with highs back in the upper 70s to low 80s by Wednesday. The next system arrives late in the week, and global guidance indicates a much better return of Gulf moisture ahead of it, so the setup should look more typical for the Southeast. Confidence in timing and details is low. PoPs stay at 20-30 percent Thursday and Friday for now.
