...16Z Update...

...North Georgia, Florida Panhandle, and Southern Appalachians...

The inherited Moderate Risk was upgraded to a High Risk across north
Georgia, including metro Atlanta, and into east-central Alabama, in
coordination with WFOs FFC, BMX, GSP, and MRX and the Southeast River
Forecast Center. The surrounding Moderate Risk was expanded south to
near Columbus and north into southeast Tennessee and the southern
Appalachians. The Slight Risk now reaches the Florida Panhandle coast
near the forecast landfall point of Hurricane Delphine, where faster
motion and higher flash flood guidance keep the threat lower despite
heavy rates.

The upgrade is driven more by antecedent conditions than by
storm-total QPF. A predecessor rain event (PRE) has been nearly
stationary since Wednesday along a stalled baroclinic zone from east
Alabama through the Atlanta metro into the South Carolina Upstate. It
has produced 7 to 11 inches of rain, including about 10 inches at
Atlanta Hartsfield-Jackson International Airport in three days. Soils
are saturated, most small streams are running bankfull, and 3-hour
flash flood guidance has fallen below 1 inch across much of the metro
and below 1.5 inches over most of north Georgia. Urban runoff in the
metro will be especially efficient.

Delphine's forward speed near 50 mph will limit storm totals at any
one point, but that matters much less with FFG this low. The 12Z HREF
and REFS show a 3 to 5 hour window of 1 to 2.5 inch per hour rates in
the inner core and the right-front quadrant between roughly 03Z and
09Z. That is enough to exceed guidance nearly everywhere along and
within 60 miles of the track. PWs of 2.3 to 2.6 inches are forecast,
above the 99th percentile for late September. The 12Z RRFS, HAFS-A/B,
AI-GFS and ECMWF-AIFS agree on the overall track and timing, and
spread in rainfall placement is mostly tied to where the center
crosses the Georgia-Alabama border.

Farther north, strong southerly flow will push up the Blue Ridge
escarpment late in the period, and upslope enhancement is likely over
the north Georgia mountains and southwest North Carolina. There, 3 to
6 additional inches on already saturated slopes could trigger flash
flooding and landslides. Considerable to catastrophic flash flooding
is expected within the High Risk, including life-threatening flooding
of urban creeks in the Atlanta metro overnight when visibility and
warning reception will be poor.
