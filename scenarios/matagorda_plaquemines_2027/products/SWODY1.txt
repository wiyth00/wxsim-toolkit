...THERE IS A HIGH RISK OF SEVERE THUNDERSTORMS ALONG THE TEXAS
AND LOUISIANA COAST FROM MATAGORDA BAY TO LOWER PLAQUEMINES PARISH...

...SUMMARY...
A concentrated coastal tornado outbreak is expected today into
tonight from Matagorda Bay through the immediate upper Texas and
Louisiana coasts to lower Plaquemines Parish. Repeated rounds of
surface-based supercells will pose a threat for several strong to
intense tornadoes, with a few potentially violent tornadoes possible.
The greatest threat will occupy a narrow corridor near the shoreline,
with a sharp decrease in tornado potential farther inland.

...SYNOPSIS...
A strong shortwave trough will move from northeastern Mexico across
the northwest Gulf and lower Mississippi Valley. A surface low will
move from southeast Texas into central Louisiana, while an associated
warm front moves inland and stalls across the immediate coastal
plain. Rain-cooled continental air will resist substantial northward
movement of the boundary. A prefrontal coastal trough and successive
weak disturbances will focus repeated convection along its warm side.
The trailing cold front will reach the coast from west to east during
the evening and overnight.

...Matagorda Bay through the upper Texas coast...
Surface dewpoints of 69-73 F, MLCAPE of 1500-2500 J/kg, and only weak
inhibition will support surface-based storms near and just inland
from the coast. Backed surface winds beneath a 55-70 kt southerly
low-level jet will yield 0-1 km storm-relative helicity of
350-550 m2/s2. Effective bulk shear of 60-80 kt and low cloud bases
will support sustained supercells and significant tornadoes.

Repeated development near the coastal trough is expected from
16-22Z. Storms reaching the warm side of the front will encounter a
particularly favorable low-level environment, while those moving
well inland will become elevated over the cool surface layer.
The greatest threat is shifted toward Matagorda, Freeport, Galveston,
the Bolivar Peninsula, and Sabine Pass. Central and northern Houston
are outside the High Risk core in this scenario, although the broader
surrounding risk still supports tornadoes and damaging winds.

...Southwest Louisiana through the lower Mississippi delta...
The corridor of favorable surface-based inflow will continue along
Cameron Parish, the Vermilion and Atchafalaya coastal basins, southern
Terrebonne and Lafourche Parishes, and lower Plaquemines Parish.
MLCAPE of 1000-2000 J/kg and strengthening low-level shear will
support strong tornadoes into the evening and overnight.

The principal threat will progress into southwest Louisiana around
19-01Z, south-central Louisiana around 22-05Z, and lower Plaquemines
around 02-08Z. The eastern extension of the High Risk assumes that
warm, surface-based inflow remains established over the delta and
that separate supercells continue to develop ahead of the main line.
Discrete or semi-discrete storms are expected for several hours
before frontal convection consolidates. A 30 percent tornado area
with Intensity 2 spans the coastal strip; a localized Intensity 3
area is retained from the upper Texas coast toward Vermilion Bay.

This is a corridor of repeated storm development, rather than the
forecast track of one supercell. Individual cells will generally move
east-northeastward and may cross into stable air inland. New cells
farther east will maintain the coastal threat, especially where
confluence and local boundary intersections favor sustained inflow.
The changing shoreline orientation near the delta makes storm mode
and the location of the surface boundary particularly consequential.

...Inland extent and forecast confidence...
The High Risk corridor is approximately 45-55 miles wide before
clipping to land. Its location reflects a narrow overlap of
surface-based instability and intense low-level shear; the shoreline
itself does not enhance tornado intensity. Inland storms will
primarily be elevated where the rain-cooled layer persists. Baton
Rouge and central New Orleans remain outside the High Risk core.

A boundary displacement of 15-25 miles would materially change which
communities lie in the greatest threat corridor. High Risk confidence
also depends on sufficient supercell coverage over land. If most
storms remain offshore, the warm front fails to reach the delta, or
cold pools force early upscale growth, the elongated High Risk would
need to be reduced. Damaging winds of 65-80 mph and hail of 1-1.75
inches are secondary threats.
